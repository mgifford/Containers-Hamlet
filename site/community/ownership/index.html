Containers Hamlet / Community / Ownership

Possible structures, not legal advice

Who owns what, and who decides?

A community land trust is one possible structure. It is not the only one and it is not legally established here. These are questions for future residents, a lawyer, an accountant, a planner and the relevant authorities.

No binding model. Nothing on this page creates a lease, membership, tenancy, ownership interest or legal relationship.

Separate the layers

Six different things that are often blurred together.
LayerWhat it could meanUnresolved
LandHeld by a trust, co-op, corporation or private owner; may be leased, never sold, or sold with conditions.Title, first nation consultation, zoning, financing and perpetuity.
Individual homeThe resident may own the container dwelling and improvements, or the trust may own and lease it.Tax, financing, insurance, removal and resale.
Homesite leaseA long-term, transferable right to occupy a defined site, with fees and conditions.Term, rent review, default, inheritance and termination.
Land-trust membershipA governance relationship with rights to vote, receive information and stand for the board.Eligibility, fees, voting weight and exit.
Residents' associationOperates shared services, budgets, maintenance and rules.Powers, enforcement, meetings, records and dispute resolution.
Shared infrastructureWater, wastewater, roads, power, fire water and the lodge may be owned by a separate entity.Cost allocation, replacement reserves and liability.

Decision-making options

Compare the tradeoffs; do not pick one by default.
ApproachStrengthRisk
ConsensusStrong buy-in and protection for minorities.Slow; can be blocked by one person; burnout.
Consent-basedFocuses on whether a proposal is good enough to try, not perfect.Needs facilitation skill and clear boundaries.
Elected boardClear accountability and operational speed.Can drift from residents; needs transparency and recall.
Delegated operationsLets a maintenance or finance lead act within a budget.Must have limits, reporting and override.
SupermajorityProtects significant decisions from a narrow majority.Can make necessary change impossible.

Whichever mix is chosen, write down who decides, how someone appeals, how records are kept, how conflicts are managed and how a resident can leave.

Protections to negotiate

  • Housing security: lease term, renewal, termination, sale of the land and change of use.
  • Transparent fees: what is included, how it changes and what happens if a resident cannot pay.
  • Accessibility accommodations: physical modifications, meeting access and personal support.
  • Dispute resolution: informal steps, mediation, appeal and records.
  • Participation: notice, speaking rights, voting, board access and minority protection.
  • Resale and succession: who can buy, at what price, and whether the community has a right of first refusal.
  • Leaving: notice, removal of the home, restoration and deposit.
  • Financial distress: hardship policy, payment plan and non-eviction alternatives.
  • Household change: birth, death, divorce, care needs and additional occupants.

What future residents and advisers must decide

  • Legal entity and jurisdiction.
  • Whether homes can be owned separately and financed.
  • Lease term, rent review, resale formula and anti-speculation rules.
  • Membership, voting, board powers and dispute resolution.
  • Short-term rental, guest and occupancy rules.
  • Shared infrastructure ownership, cost allocation and reserves.
  • Insurance, wildfire/flood liability and evacuation responsibilities.
  • Accessibility modifications and personal support boundaries.

This page is a discussion framework. It is not legal, tax, planning or financial advice.
