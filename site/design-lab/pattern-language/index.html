Designing a Hamlet: Patterns, People and Place

A Pattern Language is useful here as a set of questions about relationships — between private homes and shared places, between paths and destinations, between a room and the weather outside. It is not a rulebook, a code or a scorecard.

How to read this: the pattern number and title are referenced from the 1977 book by Christopher Alexander, Sara Ishikawa and Murray Silverstein, with Max Jacobson, Ingrid Fiksdahl-King and Shlomo Angel. Every summary, hamlet interpretation and evaluation here is new text. Pattern titles/numbers were checked against the primary source; the interpretations are design propositions, not claims about the authors’ intent. The book is copyrighted; no book text or illustrations are reproduced.

The design philosophy

Containers Hamlet starts with six private homes, a shared lodge and a fictional Canadian Shield landscape. The homes should support independence and privacy. The community should support cooperation, companionship and mutual assistance. Neither objective should dominate the other.

The aim is voluntary community: opportunities for contact without compulsory participation, and private places to withdraw without feeling excluded. The landscape is not a backdrop; ecological protection, water, biodiversity, climate resilience and maintenance shape every decision.

  1. Protect the landscape first. Buildings and infrastructure respond to existing conditions rather than forcing the land to fit an arbitrary plan.
  2. Support voluntary community. Shared spaces invite participation without making residents dependent on communal activity.
  3. Preserve meaningful privacy. Each home needs visual, acoustic and social independence.
  4. Make walking useful and pleasant. Routes connect real destinations and everyday activities.
  5. Create positive outdoor spaces. Outdoor areas are usable places, not leftover gaps.
  6. Build for diverse residents. Accessibility, mobility, sensory differences, ageing, visitors and changing households shape the design.
  7. Design for the actual climate. Winter, rain, heat, snow, smoke, wildfire, power interruptions and changing weather matter.
  8. Prefer simplicity and repairability. Avoid unnecessary technical complexity and fragile infrastructure.
  9. Evaluate materials honestly. Shipping containers are an option, not an assumed environmental advantage.
  10. Make assumptions visible. Separate conceptual design, evidence and questions requiring professional assessment.
  11. Support adaptation. Homes, shared spaces and infrastructure should accommodate changing residents and needs.
  12. Avoid false precision. Do not invent costs, energy savings, ecological benefits or accessibility conformance.

This is a conceptual, fictional project. The site is not surveyed, the buildings are not construction-ready, and the pattern language is not a compliance standard. See the design philosophy notes and pattern-language method.

Pattern 36 · Community

Degrees of Publicness

Conceptual Site plan +4 Title/number verified

Make the change from public arrival to shared, semi-private and private places gradual and legible.

In Containers Hamlet: The lane, forecourt, shared paths, lodge terrace, home porches and door thresholds should form a readable gradient. Not every route should pass directly beside a bedroom window.

Current response: The baseline has an entrance, shared lanes and trails, private homes and porches, but the transitions are not consistently modelled, named or evaluated.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Can a visitor tell which places are public, shared, semi-private and private?
  • Can a resident reach a shared facility without crossing a private room or window?
  • Are thresholds usable in snow, rain and low light?

Design alternatives

Compare the dispersed baseline with the central clearing and woodland-spine scenarios, especially where shared paths meet private porches.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

More thresholds can make a place feel welcoming or over-controlled. Signs, gates and lighting can clarify or exclude.

What is unresolved

No observational evidence of how residents, visitors or service people actually experience the edges.

Limitations

  • No post-occupancy observation
  • No privacy or lighting study

Why these patterns are linked: Publicness depends on the cluster, the small gathering place and the entrance transition; those patterns make the gradient physical.

Pattern 37 · Community

House Cluster

Conceptual Site plan +3 Title/number verified

Arrange a small number of dwellings so they form a recognisable neighbourhood while retaining private territory.

In Containers Hamlet: The six homes can remain dispersed but should be evaluated as clusters around shared paths, a glade or a service point. The lodge is not a substitute for neighbourliness.

Current response: The baseline disperses six homes across the site and places the lodge near the entrance. The current arrangement has no formal cluster evaluation.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Can residents recognise a small cluster without a gate or private road?
  • Does each home retain a private edge?
  • Are shared facilities reachable from more than one cluster?

Design alternatives

Test Scenario B (central clearing) and Scenario C (woodland spine with social pockets) against the baseline.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

Clustering shortens some infrastructure and increases casual contact, but can reduce visual privacy and concentrate disturbance.

What is unresolved

No resident preference data or measured walking distances for the alternatives.

Limitations

  • Fictional site
  • No household survey

Why these patterns are linked: A cluster needs common land and a legible publicness gradient to avoid becoming either a private compound or an unprotected shared yard.

Pattern 61 · Community

Small Public Squares

Conceptual Common land +3 Title/number verified

Provide small, enclosed outdoor gathering places at the scale of everyday encounters.

In Containers Hamlet: A widened path, a rock glade or a lodge forecourt can serve this role. A formal urban square is not assumed to be appropriate in the woodland.

Current response: The baseline has informal outdoor areas and a lodge porch, but no dedicated small gathering place.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Is there a small place to pause within a short walk of each home?
  • Does it have a sense of enclosure without becoming a trap?
  • Is it usable in winter and by people with reduced mobility?

Design alternatives

The woodland-spine scenario can add two or three small pockets rather than one central square.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

More small places spread maintenance and may fragment activity. One central place is easier to service but less private.

What is unresolved

No assessment of sun, wind, snow clearing, seating or acoustic comfort at candidate places.

Limitations

  • No microclimate study
  • No furniture or maintenance plan

Why these patterns are linked: A small square works when it is part of the publicness sequence and on a path with a real destination.

Pattern 67 · Landscape

Common Land

Evidence needed Site plan +3 Title/number verified

Set aside shared land that residents collectively use and care for, distinct from private lots and public park.

In Containers Hamlet: The community land trust should identify the glade, shoreline buffers, woodland and lodge grounds as shared land with different rules; not all land is equally public.

Current response: The fiction includes shared landscape and a community land trust, but ownership, access and stewardship are not mapped consistently.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Which land is commonly held, which is private and which is protected?
  • Who maintains each shared place?
  • Does shared use damage sensitive habitat?

Design alternatives

Scenario B gives a central common clearing; Scenario C uses a linear spine of smaller common pockets.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

More common land can support shared life but increases maintenance and may disturb habitat. Protected land may need to remain inaccessible.

What is unresolved

No legal survey, stewardship agreement or ecological assessment.

Limitations

  • Fictional parcel and ownership
  • No ecology survey

Why these patterns are linked: Common land needs site repair and positive outdoor space so shared use does not become an excuse to develop sensitive ground.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

Pattern 104 · Landscape

Site Repair

Evidence needed Site plan +4 Title/number verified

Place buildings and infrastructure where they avoid unnecessary damage and help repair already disturbed ground.

In Containers Hamlet: Test each home, path and service corridor against rock, wetland, drainage, tree roots and habitat. A buffer on a drawing is not proof that damage is avoided.

Current response: The baseline has illustrative buffers, rock and wetland constraints, and elevated foundations, but no survey or construction-impact assessment.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Is this footprint on already disturbed ground?
  • What vegetation, drainage or habitat is affected?
  • Can the damage be restored, and who is responsible?

Design alternatives

The scenario comparison should show which footprints and paths move into or out of sensitive areas.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

A compact cluster may shorten services but concentrate damage; a dispersed layout may protect one glade while fragmenting habitat elsewhere.

What is unresolved

No ecological survey, arborist assessment, drainage model or construction-access plan.

Limitations

  • Fictional landscape
  • No professional ecological assessment

Why these patterns are linked: Site repair constrains the house cluster, common land and paths; those patterns should follow the land rather than force a geometry.

Pattern 105 · Landscape

South Facing Outdoors

Evidence needed Site plan +4 Title/number verified

Put the main outdoor places on the sunny side and use the building to shelter them from wind.

In Containers Hamlet: Check porch orientation, roof overhangs and snow shedding. A lake view may justify a west or north porch, but the trade-off must be explicit.

Current response: The baseline orients homes to views and access; porch orientation is not systematically evaluated for winter sun.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Which outdoor places receive useful winter sun?
  • Are they sheltered from prevailing wind and snow?
  • Does the orientation trade a view for comfort?

Design alternatives

A scenario-level solar/wind study could test whether the lodge terrace and cabin porches have a usable winter microclimate.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

South glazing and porches gain sun but can overheat, lose heat and reduce privacy. A view orientation may outweigh solar gain.

What is unresolved

No solar, wind or snowdrift model; no measured microclimate.

Limitations

  • No site-specific solar or wind study

Why these patterns are linked: South-facing outdoor space and positive outdoor space reinforce one another; light on two sides affects the interior side of the same decision.

Pattern 112 · Building

Entrance Transition

Conceptual Individual homes +3 Title/number verified

Make arrival gradual: path, gate, porch and threshold should give people time and shelter to shift from public to private.

In Containers Hamlet: Each home needs a legible, step-free arrival sequence with room for a visitor, deliveries, snow and wet clothing. The accessible homes need the threshold detail to work.

Current response: Homes have porches and step-free entrance intent, but threshold dimensions, door clearances, visitor space and winter maintenance are not fully modelled.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Can a visitor pause out of the weather before entering?
  • Is the route step-free and clear of snow storage?
  • Can residents reach the door without passing a private window?

Design alternatives

Test a deeper covered entry or a small entrance hall in the two accessible homes.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

A generous transition improves comfort and privacy but consumes floor area and may add heat loss.

What is unresolved

No door/threshold construction detail, no snow or drainage detail.

Limitations

  • No construction detail
  • No winter maintenance plan

Why these patterns are linked: The entrance transition is where the publicness gradient becomes a physical threshold; it depends on the path and outdoor space.

Pattern 119 · Infrastructure

Arcades

Conceptual Paths +3 Title/number verified

A covered walk or gallery can connect entrances and outdoor rooms while giving shelter and a social edge.

In Containers Hamlet: Consider a covered link at the lodge or between a home and a shared workshop, not a continuous enclosed mall. Snow, ice, drainage and maintenance matter.

Current response: The baseline has porches but no shared arcade. The cold-climate idea is not tested.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Which shared destinations need weather protection?
  • Can the route be kept clear of snow and ice?
  • Does the cover create a dark or unsafe place?

Design alternatives

Test a short covered link at the lodge entrance and a sheltered waiting place on the spine.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

Cover improves winter use but adds structure, snow load, shading, maintenance and cost; it can also create dark or unsafe edges.

What is unresolved

No snow-load, drainage, daylight or maintenance assessment.

Limitations

  • No structural or snow-load design

Why these patterns are linked: An arcade is a path with a roof and a social edge; it should not substitute for a good outdoor room or a clear entrance transition.

Pattern 120 · Infrastructure

Paths and Goals

Evidence needed Site plan +4 Title/number verified

Connect meaningful destinations with paths shaped by the places they pass, not just the shortest line.

In Containers Hamlet: Map the lodge, homes, workshop, parking, drop-off and shared outdoor places. Distinguish everyday routes from recreational trails and emergency access.

Current response: The site has lanes and trails linking the entrance, homes and lodge, but accessibility, winter maintenance and destination hierarchy are not evaluated.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • What everyday destinations does this path connect?
  • Is it usable by someone with reduced mobility in winter?
  • Does it cross sensitive land or private space?

Design alternatives

Scenario C makes the spine explicit; Scenario A relies on dispersed routes; Scenario B shortens some trips but concentrates them.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

More direct paths reduce walking but may cross sensitive ground or private edges. Longer scenic routes may be pleasant but unsuitable for daily accessibility.

What is unresolved

No route-by-route grade, surface, width, lighting or winter assessment.

Limitations

  • No topographic route survey
  • No winter maintenance plan

Why these patterns are linked: Paths and goals give the publicness sequence and common land a network; an arcade is a special covered segment, not a replacement for the whole network.

Pattern 129 · Community

Common Areas at the Heart

Evidence needed Common lodge +3 Title/number verified

Put shared facilities where everyday activity passes and where people can see and reach them, not at a remote edge.

In Containers Hamlet: The lodge is near the entrance. Test whether it is socially central, whether residents pass it on ordinary walks, and whether shared facilities can be used without joining a large event.

Current response: The lodge is a single shared building near the entrance with kitchen, laundry, workshop and gathering space. Its social centrality is asserted, not measured.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Does daily life pass the common area?
  • Can a resident use one facility without joining an event?
  • Are quiet and noisy activities separated?

Design alternatives

A small shared kitchen/workshop pocket on the spine, or a satellite mailbox/tool shed, could complement the lodge.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

A central lodge supports shared activity but concentrates noise and may dominate the community. Satellite facilities add maintenance but reduce dependence on one building.

What is unresolved

No resident activity data, no acoustic or management study.

Limitations

  • No post-occupancy or resident survey

Why these patterns are linked: Common areas need a house cluster and public outdoor room to become a heart; a remote lodge alone cannot create community.

Pattern 141 · Interior

A Room of One’s Own

Conceptual Individual homes +1 Title/number verified

Give each resident a private room they can close, personalise and use without negotiating shared space.

In Containers Hamlet: Check bedroom counts and private space in each home. A one-bedroom home works for one resident or a couple; it is not a universal household model.

Current response: The six homes include one-bedroom and studio arrangements. The accessible homes have one bedroom and no dedicated guest room.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Does every resident have a door they can close?
  • Can the room be used for sleep, work or retreat?
  • Is there daylight and a place for personal belongings?

Design alternatives

Test a small alcove/workspace within the bedroom or a separate garden studio where site and budget allow.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

More private rooms increase floor area, cost and energy demand; a small home may need a flexible room instead.

What is unresolved

No household composition assumptions or furniture/clearance study.

Limitations

  • No defined household sizes
  • No furniture layout

Why these patterns are linked: A private room depends on a clear intimacy gradient and is made usable by alcoves and a window place.

Pattern 159 · Building

In Containers Hamlet: Test existing rooms for two-sided light. A 2.35 m clear width makes cross-light difficult; end windows, roof lights and open-plan connections may help but add heat loss and structural work.

Pattern 171 · Landscape

Tree Places

Evidence needed Site plan +4 Title/number verified

Preserve special trees and make places around them for sitting, play or gathering rather than clearing every tree.

In Containers Hamlet: The woodland is the hamlet’s character. Map candidate tree places, root protection zones and views; do not treat the whole site as buildable.

Current response: The site has forest, rock and buffers, but no tree survey or root-protection plan. The site plan is illustrative.

Pattern 180 · Interior

Window Place

Conceptual Individual homes +2 Title/number verified

Make a comfortable place to sit at a window, with light, a view and a sense of enclosure.

In Containers Hamlet: Use window places in the long cabins for reading, work and landscape connection. Include accessible seating and safe window operation; do not block the route.

Current response: Not modelled. Windows are recorded, but no seat or clear floor zone is assigned.

Design questions, trade-offs, evidence and limits

Questions to ask

  • Can a person sit at the window comfortably?
  • Is the seat accessible and easy to leave?
  • Is there glare, privacy or overheating?

Design alternatives

Test a built-in bench with a lift-out accessible seat, or a movable chair for flexibility.

Trade-offs and competing priorities

Built-ins save space but are hard to change and may not suit all bodies; movable furniture is flexible but can block routes.

What is unresolved

No seat height, reach, glare, privacy or window-operation study.

Limitations

  • No ergonomic or daylight study

Why these patterns are linked: A window place is a small room of one’s own and an alcove at the building edge; it connects the interior to the outdoor room and two-sided light.

A pattern may be partially applicable or deliberately rejected. There are no pass/fail scores and no single “community quality” index. Evidence states describe the current application, not the pattern’s worth.

What the evidence labels mean

Evidence states used in the Explorer.
StateMeaning
Not evaluatedThe design question has not been considered.
ConceptualA potential response has been illustrated but not tested.
Evidence neededA relevant concern is identified, but the supporting information is missing.
Evidence-supportedA conclusion is supported only within the stated scope.
Trade-off identifiedThe design improves one consideration while potentially weakening another.
Not applicableThe pattern is not relevant here, with an explanation.

Evidence states are stored on the application, not the pattern definition. The same pattern may be conceptual for one home, evidence-needed for the site and not applicable to the lodge.
