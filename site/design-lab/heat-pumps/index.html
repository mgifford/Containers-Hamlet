Residential heating and cooling

Heating without wiring the woods

A heat pump is efficient, but it is not free. This assessment keeps the existing household load and reference solar arrays unchanged and asks a narrower question: what additional electricity, panels, battery and inverter capacity would each home need for a heat pump, and could passive design or a hybrid strategy avoid it?

Preliminary assessment, not an HVAC design. The model uses canonical geometry but assumed U-values, airtightness, gains, solar factors and heat-pump performance. It is not a building-performance simulation, equipment selection, electrical design, ground-loop design, cost estimate or comfort guarantee. Confirm every result with a mechanical designer and the selected equipment data.

The short answer

For the six homes, full-time winter heat-pump heating would add roughly 30–56 panels beyond the existing reference arrays when sized for the winter-critical month. That is a large increase on arrays of 8–16 panels, and it does not include the additional battery, inverter and enclosure work.

The annual energy total alone is misleading. Summer and shoulder-season surplus can run a heat pump for cooling, dehumidification or occasional heating, and a hybrid wood/heat-pump strategy is much smaller. The winter-critical result is the one that determines whether a home can operate off-grid through a cold, cloudy week.

Recommendation: do not size the reference arrays for full-time electric heating. Treat heat pumps as a hybrid or surplus-solar service, improve the envelope and airtightness first, and keep a non-electric heating path. The Accessible Garden Home needs a specific solution because wood handling cannot be assumed.

The −30 °C case is an extreme design condition, not a typical winter day. Ottawa's 1991–2020 January mean is −9.6 °C; extreme minima near −32 °C have been recorded. A heat pump that works at −30 °C may still be operating at low capacity and low COP.

Assumptions, exclusions and reviews

  • Baseline unchanged. Household electricity and the existing reference arrays are unchanged. Heat-pump electricity is additional. No panel is removed or resized automatically.
  • Envelope. U-values: wall 0.18, roof 0.12, floor 0.15, window 0.95, door 1.2 W/m²K; thermal-bridge allowance 10%; ACH50 1.0–1.5; HRV 85%. These are assumptions, not tested performance.
  • Climate. Monthly mean temperatures are Ottawa (City) 1991–2020 normals. The −30 °C case is an extreme design condition. The model is a steady-state daily balance, not an hourly simulation.
  • Heat pump. Illustrative cold-climate COP and capacity curves informed by the NEEP cold-climate specification test points. Confirm the selected model's published table. Ground-source COP is held constant; the ground loop, boreholes and geology are not designed.
  • Excluded. Ductwork, distribution losses, domestic hot water, laundry, EV charging, cooking changes, cooling humidity, defrost water disposal, noise, refrigerant, maintenance and cost.
  • Wood heat is not universal. Stove capacity, fuel storage, physical ability, indoor air quality and chimney maintenance are not guaranteed. The Accessible Garden Home cannot rely on wood handling by a resident.
  • Required reviews. Mechanical design and heat-loss calculation; electrical design and service upgrade; structural review for additional panels and equipment; ground-source geotechnical and hydrogeological review; fire separation for battery and plant; accessibility review; cost and lifecycle assessment.

Sources: Natural Resources Canada heat-pump guidance; NRCan Keeping the Heat In; Environment and Climate Change Canada 1991–2020 climate normals for Ottawa; NEEP cold-climate air-source heat-pump specification; the project's residential energy model. See the full methodology.
